# Chapter 21. Figures that communicate

## Exercise 21.1

**Goal.** Draw a labeled volcano plot for basal virgin against basal lactating,
and report how many points it contains.

**Answer.** The number under the figure is **14,206**, four short of the 14,210
rows in `logcpm`.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

basal = samples.index[samples.cell_type == "basal"]
virgin = [s for s in basal if samples.loc[s, "stage"] == "virgin"]
lactating = [s for s in basal if samples.loc[s, "stage"] == "lactating"]

t, p = stats.ttest_ind(logcpm[lactating], logcpm[virgin], axis=1)
by_stage = pd.DataFrame(
    {"log2_fold_change": (logcpm[lactating].mean(axis=1)
                          - logcpm[virgin].mean(axis=1)),
     "p_value": p},
    index=logcpm.index,
)

print(by_stage["p_value"].isna().sum(), "genes with no p-value")

fold = by_stage["log2_fold_change"]
support = -np.log10(by_stage["p_value"])
strong = (by_stage["p_value"] < 0.05) & (fold.abs() >= 1)
print(int(np.isfinite(support).sum()), "points drawn from",
      len(by_stage), "rows")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.6, 3.3))
ax.scatter(fold[~strong], support[~strong], s=3, color="#c9c9c9", linewidths=0)
ax.scatter(fold[strong], support[strong], s=3, color="#440154", linewidths=0)
ax.axvline(-1, color="#666666", lw=0.7, ls=":")
ax.axvline(1, color="#666666", lw=0.7, ls=":")
ax.axhline(-np.log10(0.05), color="#666666", lw=0.7, ls=":")
ax.set_xlabel("log2 fold change, lactating minus virgin (log2 CPM)")
ax.set_ylabel("support, -log10(p-value)")
ax.annotate("Brca1", (fold[12189], support[12189]), xytext=(-5.5, 4.0),
            fontsize=8, arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", lw=0.6, color="k"))
fig.tight_layout()
```

```
4 genes with no p-value
14206 points drawn from 14210 rows
```

Two more numbers off the same run, so you can check the picture against them.
1,923 genes sit past both threshold lines. *Brca1*, Entrez 12189, sits at a log2
fold change of **-2.29**, which is left of center.

**Where the four missing genes went.** Their counts don't vary at all inside
either pair, so the t-test had nothing to divide by and returned `NaN`.
`by_stage["p_value"].isna().sum()` returns 4. `ax.scatter` won't complain about a
missing value. It draws nothing at that spot, carries on, and returns a figure
that looks finished. No error appears and the plot is quietly short.

**Why *Brca1* flips direction.** The chapter's own volcano compares basal against
luminal and puts *Brca1* on the right arm at +1.96, meaning higher in luminal
cells. This one compares virgin against lactating inside the basal cells only, and
there *Brca1* falls, so it lands on the left. Same gene, same count matrix, two
different comparisons. The sign belongs to the comparison and not to the gene, and
which group went first in the subtraction is recorded nowhere except in your code.
Chapter 24 explains what the flip means.

**Why the axis label has to name its units.** `log2 CPM` on the end of the x-axis
label is doing real work. Counts, counts per million and log2 CPM differ from each
other by whole orders of magnitude, so a fold change with no scale named can't be
checked by anybody.

**The most common wrong answer.** 14,210 points, written down without counting
them. It's the number of rows in `logcpm` and it's the number the chapter's own
volcano prints, and here it's wrong by four. Nothing on the figure marks the gap.
The exercise asks for the count the figure actually contains, which means counting
finite values rather than counting rows.

The second most common is `ValueError: x and y must be the same size`. You
filtered one array and not the other, so matplotlib is being asked to draw 1,923 x
positions against 14,206 y positions. `fold[strong]` goes with `support[strong]`,
never with a bare `support`.

The third is running the subtraction the other way round, `virgin` minus
`lactating`, which puts *Brca1* at +2.29 on the right arm. Nothing errors and the
figure is symmetric enough that it looks the same. The x-axis label says
`lactating minus virgin`, so the label and the arithmetic have to agree, and the
label is the only place a reader can check.

## Exercise 21.2

**Goal.** Name everything wrong with the bad figure, before opening the script
that drew it.

**Answer.** Exactly five defects. They're listed at the top of
`scripts/ch21_bad_figure.py`, and here they are with the part of the figure each
one belongs to.

| # | Part of the figure | The defect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The axes | Neither axis is labeled, so nothing on the figure says what's being measured or in what units, and the numbers up the side mean nothing. |
| 2 | The colors | The bars are red and green, which is the one pair that the commonest form of color vision deficiency collapses into each other. |
| 3 | An axis | The y axis starts at 18 rather than 0, and no part of the figure says so, which turns a six percent difference between the group means into a cliff. |
| 4 | The legend | It labels the two colors virgin and pregnant. The samples are virgin and lactating, and there's no pregnant sample in the figure at all. |
| 5 | The caption | It states that lactation halves *Brca1*. The group means are 20.5 and 19.3 counts per million, so the figure doesn't support it. |

**Why the truncated axis is a defect here and not always.** Chapter 21 opens on
two panels of the same four numbers, one starting at 0 and one starting at 18, and
says neither one is lying. A cut-off axis is legitimate and often necessary, and a
zero-based axis on a gene expressed at 20 counts per million turns a real
difference into nothing. What makes it a defect in this figure is the silence.
Seven words in the caption covers it: `y axis starts at 18, not zero`. That's the
difference between a choice and a trick.

**Why defects 4 and 5 are the valuable ones.** They're the two you walk past. The
legend one needs you to read the legend against the sample names under the bars,
which nobody does, because a legend reads as a description of the figure rather
than as a claim about it. The caption one needs you to treat the caption as a claim
and ask what would have to be true for it to hold. Halving means a factor of two.
20.5 over 19.3 is 1.06.

**The most common wrong answer.** Finding six. One of yours is a matter of taste
rather than a defect, and working out which is worth doing. The usual sixth is the
bar chart itself: four points drawn as bars hides the spread inside each pair, and
a dot plot would show it. That's a good argument and it isn't in the script's list,
because the same objection applies to most published bar charts and the exercise is
about defects with a right answer.

The second most common is finding three: the missing labels, the red and green,
and the truncated axis. Those are the three you can see without reading anything.
The other two need you to read the legend and the caption as claims that could be
false.
