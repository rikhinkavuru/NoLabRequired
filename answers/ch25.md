# Chapter 25. Interpreting results

## Exercise 25.1

**Goal.** Write the three sentences about your results that a biologist would
want, and stop at three.

**Answer.** Three sentences. Each carries at least one number, and neither of the
two that use the word significant uses it without naming the threshold and the
correction in the same sentence.

> Basal and luminal mammary cells differ across most of the transcriptome: 6,199
> of 14,210 tested genes are significant at an adjusted p-value below 0.05 under
> Benjamini-Hochberg, with six samples on each side.

> *Brca1* sits about 3.9-fold higher in luminal cells than in basal cells, a log2
> fold change of +1.96 at an adjusted p of 0.00914 under the same correction.

> Inside the basal samples *Brca1* falls from a mean of 8.3 to 0.9 counts per
> million between virgin and lactating animals. With two mice per group, only 9 of
> 14,206 genes clear an adjusted 0.05, and *Brca1* is not one of them, at an
> adjusted p of 0.0693.

**Why those three and not others.** Each one survives the check box's test. Swap
the gene name for a different gene and sentences two and three stop being true,
because both carry a number attached to Entrez 12189 specifically. Sentence one is
about the whole table on purpose and it names the count, the total and the group
sizes, so it can't be recycled onto a different comparison either.

Sentence three is the one doing the most work, and it's the one a beginner leaves
out. It reports a real fall, gives the two numbers a reader can divide, and says
plainly that the gene didn't clear the line. A gene that missed the cut isn't a
gene that stayed still. With two samples against two you had almost no chance of
showing that it moved, and that sentence is where you say so.

**What got left out, and why stopping at three matters.** No sentence about
pathways, no sentence about what *Brca1* does in humans, and no sentence about
cancer. The study these samples came from was chasing a different gene, `Mcl1`,
which is why every sample is named `MCL1-` something. *Brca1* appears in your
results because the sequencing covered every gene in the mouse. Its presence is a
fact about the dataset rather than a finding, and a fourth sentence is where that
distinction gets lost.

**The arithmetic behind the fold changes**, so a reader can redo it:

```python
print(round(2 ** 1.96, 1))
print(round(2 ** 2.29, 1))
```

```
3.9
4.9
```

A log2 fold change of +1.96 is a rise of 3.9-fold. A log2 fold change of -2.29 is
a drop of 4.9-fold. Note that the second one differs from the ratio of the two
group means, which is 8.3 over 0.9, or 9.2. Both are right. The 9.2 is a ratio of
two means and the -2.29 is a difference of logs taken after 1 was added to every
value, which lifts a small number like 0.9 far more than it lifts a large one.
Chapter 27 takes that apart. Print the method beside the number and nobody has to
work out which one you did.

**The most common wrong answer.** Calling *Brca1* significant in the
virgin-against-lactating comparison, on the raw p-value of 0.00804. Two p-value
columns sit on the same row and nothing stops you reading the wrong one. Quote
0.00804 and you've written a true sentence about a number that answers a question
nobody asked, in a table where 14,206 tests were run. The adjusted value is
0.0693, which is above 0.05. Nothing errors.

The second most common is a sentence using the word significant with nothing
beside it. "Brca1 is significantly higher in luminal cells" passes as English and
fails the exercise. Significant at which threshold, under which correction? The
same claim holds at an adjusted p of 0.00914 under Benjamini-Hochberg and it fails
under Bonferroni, whose bar here is 0.05 divided by 14,210, which is about
3.5e-06. Under Bonferroni the strongest gene in the whole
virgin-against-lactating comparison, at 4.47e-06, misses as well, so that
comparison reports nothing at all.

The third is writing more than three. The instruction to stop at three is the
exercise. A fourth sentence is almost always the one that turns a table into a
story, and it's the one that isn't in your data.
