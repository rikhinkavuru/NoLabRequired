# Chapter 29. What the field looks like

## Exercise 29.1

**Goal.** Write the systems biology question and the drug discovery question that
your own Chapter 24 result raises.

**Answer.** Two questions, one sentence each, both naming a gene by symbol and
both naming a direction. The gene at the top of the Chapter 24
basal-against-luminal table is Entrez 12293, which that chapter's symbol table
names `Cacna2d1`, at a log2 fold change of -7.26.

**The systems biology question.**

> `Cacna2d1` is 7.26 doublings lower in luminal cells than in basal cells: which
> other genes does a curated pathway database place alongside it, and do those
> genes move down across the same twelve samples too?

*The data I'd need and don't have:* a curated list of which genes belong to which
pathway. That list is a database somebody else assembled out of published
experiments, and nothing in the count matrix carries it. The matrix holds 27,179
row labels and twelve columns of numbers, and it has no column saying what any row
is connected to.

**The drug discovery question.**

> Does an experimental structure exist for the protein `Cacna2d1` encodes, at a
> resolution good enough to place a small molecule into a pocket, and does that
> structure cover the part of the chain a molecule would have to reach?

*The data I'd need and don't have:* a structure, with a stated resolution and a
stated residue range. Chapter 8 is where you find out whether one exists, and
Chapter 8 also gave you the reason the question is worth asking in that shape.
UniProt lists 33 PDB entries for human BRCA1 and every one of them covers about 11
percent of the chain, so a structure count is not coverage.

**Why neither one is answerable from the count matrix.** Both name something
outside your own table. The systems question needs a connection between genes,
which is somebody else's curation. The drug discovery question needs a shape,
which is somebody else's experiment. That's the check the exercise runs, and it's
the point of the chapter: a subfield is easier to recognize by the question it
asks than by the software it runs.

**A second pair, if you took `Krt5` instead.** Entrez 110308 sits fourth in the
same table at -8.97, and it works identically. Which other keratins move with it
across the same samples, and does a curated pathway database group them? Does a
structure exist for the protein it encodes?

**The most common wrong answer.** A question that turns out to be the genomics
question wearing new words. "Which other genes are lower in luminal cells
alongside `Cacna2d1`?" sounds like systems biology and is answerable by sorting
the column you already have. It asks which rows moved, which is what Chapter 24
answered. A systems question has to name something outside your table, a pathway
or a partner protein, and a drug discovery question has to name a structure.

The second most common is a question with no direction in it. "Is `Cacna2d1`
involved in mammary development?" contains the word involved, which is a
relationship, and no file has a column for it. Say what you'd count instead. If
the honest answer is that you don't know what you'd count, the missing part is the
measurement, and Chapter 22 is where that gets fixed.

The third is naming the gene by its Entrez ID. The exercise asks for the symbol
Chapter 24 attached to that ID, because a question is a sentence somebody else has
to read. 12293 is the right label for a table and the wrong one for a sentence.
That's the same trade Chapter 20 laid out from the other direction.
