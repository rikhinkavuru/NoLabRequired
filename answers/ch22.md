# Chapter 22. Framing a question

## Exercise 22.1

**Goal.** Rewrite a question you care about until all three parts are on the
page.

**This one genuinely varies**, because the starting sentence is yours. Here's one
worked example, and it's the one the chapter narrows in its first section.

**Four lines.**

1. *The statement I started from.* Cancer is interesting.
2. *In between.* I want to work on breast cancer genes.
3. *In between.* Is *BRCA1* expressed differently in breast cancer?
4. *The final question.* In GSE60450, do basal cells (6 samples) and luminal
   cells (6 samples) differ in *Brca1* expression, measured as the mean counts
   per million per group? I would be wrong if the two group means land within a
   factor of two of each other.

**The three parts, pointed at.**

- *Two groups with a sample count beside each.* basal, 6 samples; luminal, 6
  samples.
- *The measurement with its unit.* Mean counts per million for Entrez 12189, per
  group. Not "expression", which is a subject rather than a number.
- *The result that would prove me wrong.* The two group means landing within a
  factor of two.

**The check the exercise asks for.** Cover the line and name the file you'd open
first and the column you'd read. The file is `data/GSE60450_counts.tsv.gz`, the
row is Entrez 12189, and the columns are the twelve sample columns. The labels
that say which six are basal live in a second file,
`data/GSE60450_samples.csv`, and joining those two by key rather than by position
is Chapter 24's whole first half.

**What happened when this question was run.** The means come out at 4.4 counts
per million in the basal samples and 17.0 in the luminal ones, a factor of 3.9,
so the question survives its own falsification test. Chapter 24 tests the same
comparison properly and reports a log2 fold change of +1.96 with an adjusted
p-value of 0.00914.

**The uncomfortable part, and it belongs in the answer.** That final question
isn't about cancer any more. It's mouse mammary tissue, the symbol is spelled
`Brca1` because mouse nomenclature capitalizes differently, and nothing in the
answer will mention a tumor. If that trade is unacceptable, the repair is a
different dataset, which is Chapter 23. The repair is never a vaguer question.

**The most common wrong answer.** A final line with no numbers in it. "Do basal
cells and luminal cells differ in *Brca1* expression?" names two categories, and a
category can't be counted. The check box says your final line should carry at
least two numbers and they should be the sizes of the two groups. Pile size is
part of the question, and Chapter 24 shows what the difference between six a side
and two a side does to the answer: 6,199 genes past correction against 9.

The second most common is a measurement that reads "expression" or "level" or
"activity". Those are subjects. The test is whether you can name the file and the
column, and you can't name a column for "activity".

The third is leaving off the "I would be wrong if" line, which is the part most
people drop, including the chapter's author. It costs nothing right up to the day
you're holding a table and have no way to say whether it's the right one. Watch
for "differ" with no threshold under it: a question that can only come out one way
will come out that way on any input, including shuffled input.

## Exercise 22.2

**Goal.** Move each statement one step less vague, then name the part that's
still missing.

**Answer.** Three rewrites. These are worked examples, and the rewrite you wrote
is fine if it moved one step and you named what's still absent.

**1. Sleep affects the immune system.**

> One step: Does short sleep change the number of immune cells circulating in
> blood?

*Still missing: the comparison.* Short compared with what, and how many people on
each side? "Short sleep" is a category until somebody writes down four hours
against eight hours, twenty people against twenty.

**2. Some tumors come back after treatment and some don't.**

> One step: In a dataset where recurrence was recorded, do tumors that came back
> carry different gene expression from tumors that didn't?

*Still missing: the measurement with a unit.* "Different gene expression" names a
subject. Which number, out of which column of which file? Counts per million for
a named gene, or the count of genes past an adjusted p-value threshold, are two
different questions with two different answers.

**3. Gut bacteria have something to do with obesity.**

> One step: In a public 16S dataset with body mass index recorded, does the
> relative abundance of one named genus, as a percentage of reads per sample,
> differ between the highest and lowest BMI quartiles?

*Still missing: the direction you could be wrong about.* This one has both other
parts and no threshold under the word "differ", so it can't come out badly. Pick
the number that would change your mind, write it down before the run, and the run
gets a chance to disagree with you.

**Why one step is rarely enough.** Each rewrite above gained one part and kept
its two gaps, which is what a real narrowing looks like. Adding words to a topic
gets you a longer topic, and the three parts arrive one at a time.

**The most common wrong answer.** Three rewrites that all come out fully
answerable. That means you jumped the middle version and wrote the final question
straight off, which is the thing the exercise is trying to make visible. The check
box says at least one of your three should still be missing a part after one step.

The second most common is a measurement that reads "activity" or "level". Look at
your three measurements. If one of them is a subject where a number goes, that's
the part still missing, whatever else you named.
