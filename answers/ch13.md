## Exercise 13.1

**Goal.** Write a translator that turns any coding sequence into a protein
sequence, then prove it isn't hard-wired to one gene.

**Answer.** The translator, and two runs.

```python
def translate(dna):
    translated = ""
    for i in range(0, len(dna), 3):
        translated += codon_table[dna[i:i + 3]]
    return translated


full = translate(coding)
print(len(full), full.count("*"))

short = translate(coding[:30])
print(short, len(short), short.count("*"))
```

```
1864 1
MDLSALRVEE 10 0
```

<!-- nlr-allow: 1864 -->

On `coding`: 1,864 characters with one `*`, and `full[-1]` is that `*`. On
`coding[:30]`: 10 characters, `MDLSALRVEE`, no `*` anywhere.

The 30-base run is what proves the function isn't hard-wired. Thirty bases is ten
codons, the loop ran ten times against a table it looked every codon up in, and
nothing in the body mentions `coding`, `brca1` or *BRCA1*.

**Why 1,864 and not 1,863.** 5,592 divides by three exactly, giving 1,864
codons, and every codon becomes one character including the stop. The stop codon
gets written as `*`, which is what translation software puts where a stop sits.
Dropping it is Exercise 13.2's job, and doing it inside the translator is a
decision that costs you the check.

**Why the length has to divide by three.** A coding sequence that doesn't is
either mis-annotated or mis-sliced, and `len(coding) / 3` printing `1864.0` with
nothing after the decimal point is the cheapest place in the whole pipeline to
catch that.

**The most common wrong answer.** A translator that throws the stop away for
you, so `len` comes back 1,863 and `count("*")` comes back 0. It looks tidier and
it's a decision you made rather than one the genetic code made. Exercise 13.2
will make you undo it, because the check there is that your string equals NCBI's
character for character, and you need to know whether you removed the stop or
never had one.

The second most common is a `KeyError` on a two-letter key such as `'CA'`. Every
key in `codon_table` is three letters long, so a two-letter lookup means the loop
ran off the end of a sequence whose length doesn't divide by three. The usual
cause is translating the whole 7,088-base transcript rather than the 5,592-base
coding part.

The third is a `KeyError` on `'atg'`. The keys are uppercase and your sequence
isn't. Chapter 11 covered what lowercase costs you. Call `.upper()` on the
sequence the moment it enters your notebook.

## Exercise 13.2

**Goal.** Check your protein against `NP_009225.1`, character by character.

**Answer.** Three values.

| | |
|---|---|
| Length of your protein | 1,863 |
| Length of NCBI's | 1,863 |
| The two strings are equal | `True` |

```python
protein = translate(coding)[:-1]
print(len(protein))
print(protein[:9], protein[-10:])
print(protein.count("M"))
```

```
1863
MDLSALRVE LIPQIPHSHY
30
```

Fetch `NP_009225.1` from NCBI in FASTA, drop the header line, join the wrapped
sequence lines into one string, and compare:

```python
lines = open("np_009225.fasta").read().splitlines()
ncbi = "".join(lines[1:])
print(len(protein), len(ncbi), protein == ncbi)
```

```
1863 1863 True
```

**Why `[:-1]` and not something cleverer.** `translate` handed back 1,864
characters ending in `*`. A stop codon tells the ribosome to stop rather than
adding an amino acid. It has no place in a protein sequence, and NCBI doesn't
carry one. `translated[:-1]` drops the last character, and the last character is
the stop because your slice started in the right place.

**The check inside the check.** `protein.count("M")` comes back 30, and Chapter
12 found 30 in-frame `ATG`s by stepping through the coding range in threes. Two
different pieces of code, two routes, one answer. Every `ATG` the ribosome reads
as a word puts a methionine into the protein, so those two numbers have to match
or one of them is wrong.

**The most common wrong answer.** 1,864 against 1,863, from keeping the stop
codon. The two lengths disagree by exactly one and the strings are otherwise
identical, so the comparison comes back `False` for a reason that has nothing to
do with your translation.

The second is a protein much shorter than 1,863, usually 59 residues. That comes
from slicing at base 1 instead of base 114, meaning you translated the
untranslated front end of the transcript, which isn't in frame with anything and
runs into a stop codon after 59 residues. Print the first three characters of
whatever you translated. A coding sequence starts with `ATG`, so a protein
starting with anything other than `M` is the tell.

The third case is the interesting one: the two lengths agree and the strings
don't. That means exactly one codon translated to the wrong letter, which almost
always means one row of the codon table is wrong. Print the first position where
they differ, work out which codon produced it, and go and look at that row in
`data/codon_table_standard.tsv`. This is the whole argument for loading the table
from a file rather than typing 64 rows from memory: one wrong row puts a wrong
letter at every place that codon appears, buried in 1,863 characters nobody was
ever going to read.

```python
for i in range(len(protein)):
    if protein[i] != ncbi[i]:
        print(i, protein[i], ncbi[i], coding[i * 3:i * 3 + 3])
        break
```
