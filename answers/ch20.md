# Chapter 20. Tabular data with pandas

## Exercise 20.1

**Goal.** Rank all 27,179 genes by how much they vary across the twelve samples,
once on CPM and once on the raw counts, then say what changed.

**Answer.** Two ordered lists of ten Entrez Gene IDs, most variable first, and
one number.

**Ranked on CPM:**

```
12993, 12991, 12990, 14663, 22373, 22041, 12994, 11475, 20750, 17880
```

**Ranked on the raw counts:**

```
12993, 12991, 12990, 14663, 22373, 22041, 12994, 20750, 11475, 17880
```

**How many appear in both lists: ten.**

The code:

```python
sd_cpm = cpm.std(axis=1)
sd_raw = counts[sample_cols].std(axis=1)

top_cpm = counts.loc[sd_cpm.sort_values(ascending=False).index[:10],
                     "EntrezGeneID"].tolist()
top_raw = counts.loc[sd_raw.sort_values(ascending=False).index[:10],
                     "EntrezGeneID"].tolist()

print(top_cpm)
print(top_raw)
print(len(set(top_cpm) & set(top_raw)))
```

**What changed between the two rankings.** Exactly one adjacent pair traded
places. 11475 is eighth on CPM and ninth on the raw counts, and 20750 does the
reverse. Nothing else moved.

Look at 20750's twelve counts and the reason is on the page:

| Sample | Count | | Sample | Count |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MCL1-DG | 542 | | MCL1-LA | 30,008 |
| MCL1-DH | 557 | | MCL1-LB | 30,907 |
| MCL1-DI | 5,243 | | MCL1-LC | 143,968 |
| MCL1-DJ | 3,503 | | MCL1-LD | 182,889 |
| MCL1-DK | 3,673 | | MCL1-LE | 473,398 |
| MCL1-DL | 3,573 | | MCL1-LF | 536,990 |

It peaks in `MCL1-LE` and `MCL1-LF`, and those are the two deepest columns in the
file at 24,723,827 and 24,657,293 reads. Dividing by library size takes more off
a gene that peaks in the deepest columns than off one that doesn't, so 20750
slips a place when you normalize and 11475 takes it.

**Why the two lists barely differ, and why that isn't reassuring.** The range of
library sizes here runs from 20,015,386 to 24,723,827:

```
24,723,827 / 20,015,386 = 1.235
```

A spread of about a quarter. Normalizing can only move a ranking by about as much
as the depths differ, so with a spread this narrow it moves one pair. Run the same
ranking on a study where one sample came back at half the depth of another and
the two lists come apart. Nothing in either list tells you which situation you're
in, which is why you normalize first every time rather than checking afterwards
whether it mattered.

**The gene worth looking up.** The fourth ID on both lists is 14663, and Chapter
24's symbol table names it `Glycam1`. It's also the gene sitting fourth in the
basal virgin-against-lactating results with a log2 fold change of +7.26, which is
a rise of about 150-fold in the lactating animals. Look up the three IDs at the
top, 12993, 12991 and 12990, on NCBI Gene the way Chapter 5 showed you. Three
consecutive Entrez IDs is what a family of related genes deposited together looks
like, and the question the chapter closes on is whether their records fit animals

**The most common wrong answer.** Two lists that disagree on more than that one
pair. The check gives you the first move: print `cpm.sum()` and confirm every
column reads 1,000,000. Each column was divided by its own total and multiplied by
a million, so a column reading anything else means you divided by the wrong thing,
usually by the whole table's sum instead of by each column's.

The second most common is including the `EntrezGeneID` and `Length` columns in the
standard deviation. Both are numbers, both sit in the table, and neither is a
sample. `Length` runs into the hundreds of thousands for some genes, so a row's
standard deviation computed across 14 columns instead of 12 is mostly a statement
about gene length. `counts[sample_cols]` is what keeps them out.

The third is `.std(axis=0)`, which gives you one number per sample instead of one
per gene. Twelve numbers come back instead of 27,179, and it runs cleanly.
`axis=1` means along the row. Say the direction out loud each time you type it.
