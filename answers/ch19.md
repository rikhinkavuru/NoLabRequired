# Chapter 19. Multiple alignment and trees

## Exercise 19.1

**Goal.** Find the grouping in the five-species tree that disagrees with NCBI
Taxonomy, and say what produced it.

**Answer.** Four things.

**1. The pair the tree groups together wrongly: mouse and chicken.** Here's the
Newick string from `data/alignment/brca1_cds_five_species.phylotree`, rewrapped
so the grouping shows:

```
((human:0.00399,chimpanzee:0.00316):0.07035,
 (mouse:0.15374,chicken:0.38062):0.04650,
 dog:0.08637);
```

Read the parentheses from the inside out. `(mouse,chicken)` is a group of two,
hanging from a branch of length 0.04650.

**2. The clade NCBI Taxonomy uses to contradict it: Euarchontoglires.** Human,
chimpanzee and mouse all sit inside it. Dog doesn't, because dog is Carnivora
inside Laurasiatheria. Chicken is Aves, so it sits outside Mammalia entirely. So
mouse shares a clade with the primates that dog doesn't share, and chicken is
outside all four. The tree has a branch with mouse and chicken on one side and
human, chimpanzee and dog on the other, and no rooting of it puts mouse with the
primates.

**3. The two branch lengths that explain it: mouse at 0.15374 and chicken at
0.38062.** They're the two largest in the file, and it isn't close. Test them
against the other three:

```
human to the middle of the tree:      0.00399 + 0.07035 = 0.07434
chimpanzee to the middle of the tree: 0.00316 + 0.07035 = 0.07351
dog:                                  0.08637
```

Everything else is under 0.09. The two primates only look short because they
share a stem, so you add their two numbers to get out to the middle.

**4. What a distance method cannot see.** It keeps one number per pair and
throws the alignment away, so it can't tell a site that changed once apart from a
site that changed three times and landed back on the letter it started with.

**Why the long branches produce the wrong pair.** DNA has four letters, so two
unrelated sequences still agree at about one column in four:

```
100 / 4 = 25 percent
```

Mouse and chicken read 46.56 percent identical against that floor of 25. A long
branch means many changed sites, and when a site changes on two separate branches
there's a real chance both landed on the same letter, producing a match neither
species inherited. The faster a branch runs, the more of those it manufactures.
Two fast branches manufacture them with each other, and a distance method reads
them as evidence and pulls the pair together. The effect has a name,
long-branch attraction, and Joseph Felsenstein worked out the conditions for it
in 1978.

**The most common wrong answer.** Naming dog and human as the problem, because
the identity matrix puts dog closer to human than mouse is:

```
human to dog:   83.90
human to mouse: 73.18
       gap:     10.72 percentage points
```

That gap is real and it isn't a disagreement with taxonomy. Percent identity
reports how much change has piled up, not how recently two lineages split, and
change piles up faster in some lineages than others. The tree's *topology* puts
human, chimpanzee and dog on one side, which is what taxonomy contradicts. Look
at which sequences group with which, and let the pairwise numbers go.

The second most common is picking a branch that isn't long. If one of your two
numbers is smaller than 0.08637, you've picked a branch that doesn't explain
anything, and long is the whole explanation.

The third is reaching for rooting as the fix. A root puts a direction on branches
that are already there. This tree has three items at the top level, so it's
unrooted and carries no direction of time, and rooting it would leave the
mouse-with-chicken branch exactly where it is.

## Exercise 19.2

**Goal.** Predict what adding the frog does to the tree, then read the tree that
has it in.

**Answer.** Three things, in order.

**1. The predicted topology, in words.** Here's one example prediction, and it's
the one most readers write. The frog is an outgroup to everything else, so it
comes off on its own. Chicken joins the four mammals as their nearest relative.
And inside the mammals, the mouse finally lands with human and chimpanzee where
Euarchontoglires says it belongs. The prediction is the exercise, so write yours
down before opening the file.

**2. The actual grouping in the file.**
`data/alignment/brca1_cds_with_outgroup.phylotree`, rewrapped:

```
(((human:0.00400,chimpanzee:0.00315):0.06912,
  dog:0.08760):0.05016,
 mouse:0.15008,
 (chicken:0.27050,frog:0.28095):0.10890);
```

Three groupings, read from the inside out:

- `(human, chimpanzee)` together, then `dog` joining that pair.
- `(chicken, frog)` together.
- `mouse` on its own at the top level.

Still three items at the top level, so this tree is unrooted as well, and it
carries no support numbers on any branch.

**3. Which part of the prediction was wrong.** The mammal half. The outgroup did
its job: chicken and frog come out as a pair, and the four mammals sit together on
the other side of the branch leading to them, so the mouse-with-chicken error
from Exercise 19.1 is gone. What replaced it is a different error. Mouse now sits
outside dog and the two primates, and NCBI Taxonomy puts mouse *with* the
primates in Euarchontoglires and dog further out in Laurasiatheria. One error got
fixed and one didn't, and nothing in the file marks either.

**Why the frog fixed one error and not the other.** Adding a sixth sequence gave
the chicken branch something to attract other than the mouse. Chicken's own branch
shortened from 0.38062 to 0.27050 in the new alignment, because the frog supplies
intermediate states at columns where chicken looked unique before. Mouse
stayed long at 0.15008, which is still the longest branch among the mammals by a
wide margin, so the same mechanism keeps pushing it away from the primates. An
outgroup is not a general repair for long-branch attraction. It fixed the
grouping the outgroup was adjacent to.

**The most common wrong answer.** Opening the file before writing the prediction.
The check box calls the prediction most of the exercise, and reading the answer
first removes the only thing being measured.

The second is calling the outgroup a win and stopping. Chicken and frog paired up,
the mammals came together, and the tree looks like a repair. It's half a repair.
Read the mammals before you write it down, which is what the check box asks for.

The third is reading `mouse:0.15008` sitting alone at the top level as the tree
saying mouse is the outgroup. It isn't saying that. An unrooted tree has no
oldest node, and the three top-level items are the three directions away from one
internal branching point. What the string claims is which sequences group with
which, and nothing more.
