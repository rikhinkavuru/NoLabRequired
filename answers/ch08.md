# Chapter 8. Proteins without a microscope

## Exercise 8.1

**Goal.** Find the predicted structure for `P38398` and locate one named domain
in it by residue number.

**Answer.** Three values. Taking BRCT 1 as the domain:

| Value | Answer |
|---|---|
| AlphaFold entry identifier | `AF-P38398-F1` |
| Residue range of the domain | 1642 to 1736 |
| Confidence band across it | one of the two top bands |

The model on the page as this book was written is version 6, created on 1 August
2025, and it covers all 1,863 residues. Write the version down beside the band
you read, the same way you wrote down the `.4` in `NM_007294.4`.

The other two domains work identically if you picked one of them:

```
Zinc finger   24-65        RING-type
Domain        1642-1736    BRCT 1
Domain        1756-1855    BRCT 2
```

On the band: this book's fact registry records the entry identifier, the model
version and the sequence length for `AF-P38398-F1`. It doesn't record a
per-residue pLDDT, and models get recomputed, so read the band off the
confidence plot on your own page rather than off this one. What you can predict
before you look is which band it falls in, and the prediction is very high or
confident, with the last residue or two at each edge of a domain the likeliest
place to slip a band.

**Why you can predict it.** A domain is a stretch that folds on its own into the
same shape it holds inside the full protein. That's what makes it crystallizable,
and all three of BRCA1's annotated domains have been solved experimentally, which
means structures very like them sat in the training data. A predictor asked about
a well-behaved domain is being asked the easiest question it ever gets. Low
confidence is what the model reports where a stretch has no single fixed shape,
which is the middle of this protein and not its domains.

**The most common wrong answer.** Landing in the low or very low band over an
annotated domain. That has one cause almost every time, and the check names
it: you're counting residues from somewhere other than the start of the chain.
The numbers in the block above are positions along the full 1,863-residue chain,
with the first amino acid numbered 1. A PDB entry holds a fragment and is free to
number its own residues however it likes, so a residue number taken off a
structure entry and used against the UniProt chain lands in the wrong place and
looks perfectly reasonable when it gets there.

The second is writing `P38398` in the identifier row. That's the UniProt
accession for the protein. The AlphaFold entry built from it has its own
identifier, `AF-P38398-F1`, and the `-F1` is the fragment number.

## Exercise 8.2

**Goal.** Put the AlphaFold model of BRCA1 next to `1JNX` and say what each one
covers that the other doesn't.

**Answer.** Four things.

**1. Residues in the BRCT block.** The block runs from 1,642 to 1,855:

```
1,855 - 1,642 + 1 = 214 residues
```

**2. That number as a fraction of the whole protein.**

```
214 / 1,863 = 0.115
```

About 11 percent. So 1,863 minus 214 leaves 1,649 residues of BRCA1 that `1JNX`
holds no atom of.

**3. What `1JNX` tells you that the model can't.** Its coordinates are a
measurement. Somebody grew a crystal, fired X-rays through it, and fitted a
model into the density that came back, so every atom position is constrained by
data collected off real molecules. The entry also carries a resolution, 2.5
angstroms, which is a stated blur width you can hold every coordinate against.
The AlphaFold model has no equivalent number. Its pLDDT is the network's
confidence in its own answer, out of the same network that produced the
coordinates, and no experiment enters into it.

**4. What the model shows that `1JNX` doesn't.** The other 1,649 residues. The
whole middle of the chain, where no experiment has ever placed an atom, plus the
RING-type zinc finger at the far end. Some of that middle is annotated as
disordered, including the stretch from residue 230 to residue 270, and the model
draws it anyway with a low confidence band over it. That band is the useful
output there. It's the model declining to give an answer, in a form you can read.

**Why the 11 percent is the point.** UniProt lists 33 PDB entries for this
protein, and 33 sounds like coverage. It isn't. X-ray crystallography needs
millions of copies of a molecule stacked in the same orientation, so it reports
on the parts of a protein that hold still. Those parts are the domains, and the
domains are 256 of 1,863 residues once you add the 42-residue zinc finger to the
214-residue BRCT block. A protein with 33 structures can be one small
well-behaved domain solved over and over with different partners bound to it.
Hemoglobin subunit beta makes the same point from the other end: 147 amino acids,
340 PDB entries.

**The most common wrong answer.** Writing something near 100 percent for the
fraction. It comes from using the length of the model as the denominator, or
from reading "the AlphaFold model covers 1,863 residues" as the size of what
`1JNX` covers. The two numbers in the division are the size of the experimental
structure over the size of the protein, and the whole exercise exists to make you
notice that those are different.

The second most common is 213, from dropping the plus one. That's fencepost
counting, and it's the same off-by-one as Chapter 5's coding range. A range that
includes both of its ends holds stop minus start plus one items, and the plus one
is the first end.
