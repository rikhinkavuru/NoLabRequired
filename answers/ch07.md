# Chapter 7. What the results actually mean

## Exercise 7.1

**Goal.** Rank the four searches by how much biology you can conclude from each
one, and name the column that decided each position.

**Answer.** Strongest first:

1. **The BRCA1 search** (`brca1_human_vs_nonhuman`)
2. **The fillet search** (`mystery_fillet_1_nt`)
3. **The fragment search** (`brca1_fragment_28bp`)
4. **The shuffled search** (`shuffled_control_nt`)

One sentence per search, each naming the column that put it there.

1. **BRCA1, decided by the bit score.** Its top hit scores 12,746 bits, which is
   246 times the fragment's 51.8, and that score came out of 7,029 identities
   across 7,089 aligned columns rather than out of a short perfect stretch.
2. **Fillet, decided by the bit score.** 1,199 bits over 649 of 649 positions
   puts it a long way clear of the fragment and a long way below BRCA1, and the
   description column backs it up with 49 of 50 rows naming one genus.
3. **Fragment, decided by the bit score.** 51.8 bits at `7e-04` is the weakest
   real alignment in the set, and its 100 percent identity comes from 28 out of
   28, a denominator you can read off the `Length=28` line above the table.
4. **Shuffled, decided by the hit count.** Zero rows, so no column in the table
   has anything to decide.

**The other defensible ordering.** Swapping the top two is allowed, and only if
your sentence names the column that separates them. Put the fillet search first
and the column is the description column, the one carrying the species names.
That search answered a question somebody asks in the real world, which fish is
in the packet, off 49 independent records agreeing. The BRCA1 search told you
that a chimpanzee has a *BRCA1*, which nobody doubted. Either order is fine.
What isn't fine is an order with no named column under it.

**Why the fragment can't move up.** Its 100 percent identity is honest: 28 out
of 28 really is 100 percent. Percent identity has no way to see how much
sequence went into it, so a perfect match over 28 bases and a 99 percent match
over 7,089 print in the same column, in the same units, one above the other.
Query coverage doesn't rescue you either, because coverage is a fraction of the
query you asked about. Ask about 28 bases and a full-length answer covers 28
bases, so the fragment reports 100 percent coverage as well. Work out what those
28 bases are as a fraction of the transcript they were cut from:

```
28 / 7,088 = 0.0039
```

100 percent of the query and 0.4 percent of the transcript. Both true. The bit
score is the only one of the four numbers that grows with how much sequence
agreed, which is why it's the column that ranks these correctly.

**Why the shuffled search goes last on a count rather than a score.** There's
nothing to score. The report is four lines and holds no table. That's the point
of running it: a search that can come back empty is a search whose hits are
worth reading.

**The most common wrong answer.** Putting the fragment search above one or both
of the other two, and it's wrong the same way every time. You ranked by percent
identity. That column reads 100, 100, 99 and nothing for the four searches, so
sorting by it ties the fragment for first place against a hit scoring 246 times
higher. Nothing errors and nothing warns, because the column you sorted by is
the one column in the table that can't see length. If that's what happened,
reread the two fragment sections and then look at the `Length=` line above each
table, which is the number that decides what identity is worth.

The second most common is ranking by E-value. Two of the four reports print
`0.0` in that column, and `0.0` is not zero. It means the number came out below
the smallest value the report can print. All 50 hits in the BRCA1 report print
`0.0` while their bit scores run from 12,746 down to 12,033, so you can't order
anything by a column that has hit its floor. The E-value also moves with the
size of the database, so it isn't comparable between two searches at all. The
chapter's own worked example makes the point: shrink the database by a factor of
a thousand on paper and the fragment's E-value falls from `7e-04` to `7e-07`
without one base moving.
