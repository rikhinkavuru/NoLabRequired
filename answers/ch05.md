# Chapter 5. Reading a gene

## Exercise 5.1

**Goal.** Retrieve the human *BRCA1* record and write down five values the
database actually states.

**Answer.**

| Value | What the record says |
|---|---|
| Chromosome | 17, at cytogenetic band 17q21.31 |
| Genomic span | 126,033 bases of `NC_000017.11` |
| MANE Select transcript | `NM_007294.4` |
| Transcript length | 7,088 bases |
| Protein length | 1,863 amino acids (`NP_009225.1`) |

The gene runs from 43,170,326 down to 43,044,294, and the start being bigger
than the stop is the record telling you the gene sits on the minus strand. The
span follows from those two numbers:

```
43,170,326 - 43,044,294 + 1 = 126,033
```

The protein length follows from the coding range, which the record gives as
`114..5705`:

```
5,705 - 114 + 1 = 5,592 bases
5,592 / 3        = 1,864 codons
1,864 - 1        = 1,863 amino acids
```

<!-- nlr-allow: 1864 -->

The Gene ID, if you want a sixth thing to write down, is 672.

**Why.** Every one of those five values sits on a different part of the record
and answers a different question. The span is a property of the gene as a piece
of chromosome. The transcript length is a property of one deposited sequence.
The protein length is a property of what that sequence codes for. The two
subtractions above are the only arithmetic in the exercise, and both of them
carry a plus one because a database range includes both of its ends.

The version suffix on `NM_007294.4` is the part that turns a download into a
method. `NM_007294` means whatever is current today. `NM_007294.4` means 7,088
specific bases in a specific order, forever.

**The most common wrong answer.** Writing the transcript length in the span row:
7,088 where 126,033 belongs. It happens because the transcript is the thing you
end up clicking through to, and both numbers are described as a length in bases.
The check catches it: the span is a six-figure number and the transcript length
is a four-figure one. If yours are the same order of magnitude, you recorded one
thing twice. The two differ by a factor of about eighteen, and the difference is
introns.

The second most common is dropping the `.4`. It costs you nothing today and it
costs the next person everything, because they can't fetch the same bases from
what you wrote down.

The third is 1,864 for the protein length, from stopping the arithmetic one step
early. 1,864 is the codon count. One of those codons is the stop codon, which
tells the ribosome to stop rather than adding an amino acid, so it isn't in the
protein.

## Exercise 5.2

**Goal.** Find the same gene in mouse, record the same five values, and name the
largest proportional difference.

**Answer.**

| Value | Human | Mouse |
|---|---|---|
| Symbol | `BRCA1` | `Brca1` |
| Chromosome | 17 (17q21.31) | 11 (band 11 D) |
| Genomic span | 126,033 bases of `NC_000017.11` | 63,222 bases of `NC_000077.7` |
| Transcript | `NM_007294.4` | `NM_009764.3` |
| Transcript length | 7,088 bases | 6,648 bases |
| Protein | `NP_009225.1`, 1,863 aa | `NP_033894.3`, 1,812 aa |

The mouse gene runs from 101,442,807 down to 101,379,586, so it's on the minus
strand as well:

```
101,442,807 - 101,379,586 + 1 = 63,222
```

Its coding range is `207..5645`, which checks against the protein length the
same way the human one did:

```
5,645 - 207 + 1 = 5,439 bases
5,439 / 3       = 1,813 codons
1,813 - 1       = 1,812 amino acids
```

**The largest proportional difference is the genomic span.** The human gene
occupies 126,033 bases of chromosome and the mouse gene occupies 63,222, so
126,033 divided by 63,222 is 1.99. The mouse gene fits into almost exactly half
the chromosome. Set that beside the protein:

```
1,812 / 1,863 = 0.973
```

The mouse protein is 2.7 percent shorter. Whether it surprises you is your call,
and the honest answer for most readers is yes, because a gene half the size
producing a protein the same size sounds like a contradiction.

**There is no MANE Select flag on the mouse record.** MANE is an agreement
between NCBI and EMBL-EBI over one representative transcript per *human* gene,
matching perfectly between RefSeq and Ensembl. It doesn't cover mouse. Chapter
5's If-it-breaks section gives the convention for a species with no MANE flag:
take the longest curated transcript, the one with an `NM_` prefix, and say which
one you took. `NM_009764.3` is that transcript here.

**Why the two facts aren't in tension.** Most of a gene's genomic span is
introns and the untranslated ends, and neither of those has to keep its length
for the gene to work. An intron can gain or lose a thousand bases and the
protein comes out unchanged, so intron length drifts freely between species. The
coding part can't drift that way. Remove one or two bases from a coding sequence
and every codon downstream shifts, which produces a different protein and usually
an early stop. Chapter 18 measures exactly this: all 33 gaps between the human
and mouse coding sequences are whole numbers of codons, which is what survives.

So the span is free to halve and the protein isn't. Same observation, two
different constraints.

**The most common wrong answer.** Naming the transcript length as the largest
proportional difference. It's the one the eye lands on, because 7,088 against
6,648 is a visible gap on the page, and both numbers sit in the same place on
their respective records. Do the division: 6,648 over 7,088 is 0.938, a 6
percent difference, against a factor of 1.99 on the span. The right comparison
is a ratio, and reading down the column instead of dividing hides which gap is
big.

The second most common is searching `BRCA1` and taking a mouse record off the
hit list without noticing the symbol on it. Mouse writes `Brca1`, with one
capital, enforced by a different nomenclature committee from the human one. It's
a real convention, not a typo on the page, and Chapter 20 shows you what it does
to a string comparison.
