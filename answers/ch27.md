## Exercise 27.1

**Goal.** Write the brief for your replication. One paper, one panel, one
accession.

**The paper is yours**, so here's the brief for the one paper this book already
uses, with three of its four lines filled exactly and one left open on purpose.

**Four lines.**

```
Paper:     Fu NY et al., Nature Cell Biology 2015. PubMed 25730472.
Panel:     <figure and panel, from the paper in front of you>
Accession: GSE60450
File size: 513,592 bytes (the processed count table, compressed)
```

The panel line is the one I won't fill for you, and the reason matters. This
book's fact registry holds the PubMed ID and the accession for that paper, and it
doesn't hold the paper's figure numbering. Putting a figure number here that I
haven't checked would be exactly the thing Chapter 5 spent a page telling you not
to do. Open the paper, pick a panel, and write it down.

**How to pick the panel.** Two rules, and both of them cut down the list fast.

- One panel. Not the paper, not the figure. Panel B of Figure 3, and nothing
  else.
- A panel whose axes you can name from the caption alone. If you can't say what
  runs across and what runs up, and in what units, you can't tell whether your
  version matches.

**The ten-minute test.** Fill the accession line from the paper alone, inside ten
minutes. If you can't, the paper is the wrong paper. Put it down and pick another.
A paper that never says where its data lives isn't a replication target, whatever
else it is, and there are more papers than there are Tuesdays.

**Where the accession usually is.** A data availability statement, near the end,
usually one sentence long. Failing that, the methods section, in the sentence
about sequencing. Searching GEO's free text for the paper's topic is the slow
route, because GEO search runs over words submitters chose and submitters don't
agree on words.

**The most common wrong answer.** A brief with no file size on it. That's the
line that turns the brief into a decision rather than an intention, and it's the
one people skip because it feels like bookkeeping. A processed count table is half
a megabyte. The raw reads behind the same experiment are hundreds of times bigger,
and nothing in this book can process one. Writing the size down is how you find
out which of those two you just committed to.

The second most common is picking a whole figure. Six panels is six replications,
each with its own filters and its own axis units, and the ninety-day plan in this
chapter gives you weeks 6 to 9 for one of them.

The third is picking a paper you can't read the introduction of. The exercise says
a subject you can already read the introduction of, and that's a real constraint
rather than encouragement. A difference log is a list of reasons your number
differs from theirs, and you can't write a reason for a result you can't
interpret.

## Exercise 27.2

**Goal.** Run the walkthrough cell again on `Krt5` and account for what changed.

**Answer.** Two numbers and a sentence.

| | `Brca1` (12189) | `Krt5` (110308) |
|---|---|---|
| basal virgin, mean CPM | 8.3 | 2274.3 |
| basal lactating, mean CPM | 0.9 | 3766.3 |
| ratio of the means | 9.4 | 0.6 |
| log2 fold change as a ratio | 4.9 | 0.6 |

The two routes agree to one decimal place for `Krt5`, where for `Brca1` they were
nearly a factor of two apart.

```python
krt5 = cpm.loc[110308]
basal = samples[samples["cell_type"] == "basal"]
virgin = krt5[basal[basal["stage"] == "virgin"]["sample"]]
lactating = krt5[basal[basal["stage"] == "lactating"]["sample"]]

logfc = np.log2(virgin + 1).mean() - np.log2(lactating + 1).mean()
print("basal virgin, mean CPM   ", round(virgin.mean(), 1))
print("basal lactating, mean CPM", round(lactating.mean(), 1))
print("ratio of those two means ", round(virgin.mean() / lactating.mean(), 1))
print("log2 fold change         ", round(logfc, 2))
print("the same, as a ratio     ", round(2 ** logfc, 1))
```

```
basal virgin, mean CPM    2274.3
basal lactating, mean CPM 3766.3
ratio of those two means  0.6
log2 fold change          -0.73
the same, as a ratio      0.6
```

**The prediction.** The exercise asks whether the two routes will agree more
closely than they did for `Brca1`, or less. More closely, and it isn't marginal.

**The sentence: the cause of the gap, and why it nearly vanishes here.** The log
route adds 1 to every value before taking the log, so that a gene sitting at zero
doesn't produce infinity. That 1 is a fixed size and the numbers it gets added to
are not. `Brca1`'s lactating mean is 0.9 counts per million, so adding 1 more than
doubles it, and the number that doubled is the denominator of the ratio. That's the
entire distance between 9.4 and 4.9. `Krt5` sits in the thousands, so adding 1 to
3766.3 moves it by about one part in four thousand and changes nothing you can see
at one decimal place.

**One more line for the log.** `Krt5` goes up between virgin and lactating basal
cells, not down, which is why both of its numbers are below 1 while `Brca1`'s are
above. The raw counts underneath are 58,852 and 43,881 in the two virgin samples
against 87,007 and 69,881 in the two lactating ones. `Brca1`'s are 163 and 210
against 20 and 17. Small counts are exactly where two ordinary routes come apart.

**The most common wrong answer.** Two numbers still nearly a factor of two apart.
That means you changed the Entrez ID in one place and left it alone in another,
usually changing it in the `cpm.loc[...]` line and leaving an old variable feeding
the log calculation. Both numbers should move together or neither should.

The second is a lactating mean near 19, which means you selected luminal cells
rather than basal ones. That's the Chapter 24 mistake arriving on schedule, and it
comes from filtering on the stage and forgetting the cell type.

The third is `KeyError: 'Krt5'`. The matrix is indexed on Entrez IDs and not on
symbols, so use `110308`, as an integer and not as text. Chapter 20 is where the
argument for that lives, and the short version is that no spreadsheet turns 110308
into a date.
