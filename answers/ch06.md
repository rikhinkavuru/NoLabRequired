# Chapter 6. What is this sequence?

## Exercise 6.1

**Goal.** Identify `mystery_fillet_1` and write down the evidence rather than
the answer.

**Answer.** Three things.

1. **The species, as two words:** *Gadus chalcogrammus*. In English, Alaska
   pollock.
2. **The accession of the highest-scoring hit, with its version suffix:**
   `AB182303.1`, at 1,199 bits with 649 of 649 identities and no gaps.
3. **One sentence on why more than one record supports it:** 49 of the 50 hits
   carry the name *Gadus chalcogrammus*, deposited by different people from
   different fish, so deleting the top row leaves the identification exactly
   where it was. (Twenty-seven of those rows tie at the top score with 649 of 649
   identities.)

Two more numbers worth having in your notes, because they're what the sentence
above rests on. Thirty-three of the 50 rows match all 649 positions. Another 17
print as 100 percent in the Max Ident column and are really 648 of 649, because
that column rounds to a whole number.

The one row that disagrees is `AM489719.1`, whose free-text title says
*Theragra finnmarchica*. Ask NCBI Taxonomy which species that record belongs to
today and it answers `Gadus finnmarchicus`. The title is frozen at the day it
was deposited and the taxonomy behind it is maintained, so the two disagree and
nothing on the page flags it.

**Why.** Nobody can reason from 649 bases to a species. What the search does is
ask whether anyone has ever deposited a sequence like this one and then show you
what they called it. That means the evidence is a count of independent agreeing
records, not a single row. Those 649 bases come from COI, a mitochondrial gene
that people have been depositing on purpose as a species tag since 2003, which is
why a hit list of 50 fills up with one genus instead of coming back empty.

The version suffix matters for the same reason it mattered in Chapter 5.
`AB182303` means whatever is current. `AB182303.1` means the exact bases that
produced your 1,199 bits.

**The most common wrong answer.** Copying the species off row one and stopping.
It reads as reasonable, because row one is at the top and the top is where the
best answer goes. Twenty-seven rows in this report tie at 1,199 bits with 649 of
649 identities, and the order among tied rows is not a ranking. Any of the 27
could have printed first. You've copied a name out of a tie, and it happens to be
the right name here, which is worse: the method that produced it would have given
you the wrong name on a report where the tie contained two species.

Two others, both cheap to catch:

- Writing `AB182303` without the `.1`. The check box says the accession should
  carry a dot and a number.
- Writing *Theragra finnmarchica*, off row five. It's a real name on a real
  record and it's forty-nine rows out of fifty away from the answer.

## Exercise 6.2

**Goal.** Run the negative control, then say what a search with no hits is
telling you.

**Answer.** Three items.

**Your prediction.** Written before the run. The honest prediction, and the one
most readers make, is that hits will come back, because `shuffled_control` has
the same length and the same count of every base as a query that filled its whole
hit list. That prediction is wrong, and finding out why is the exercise.

**The result.** The whole report:

```
Query= shuffled_control

Length=649

No significant similarity found.
```

Zero hits, against 128,547,243 sequences and 948,086,538,397 letters.

**Which of the three readings applies.** The first one: the sequence is unlike
anything deposited. The other two are ruled out by checks, and naming the check
is the part the exercise wants.

- *Ruled out: the wrong database.* Put
  `data/sequences/cod_reference_MT456169.fasta` through the identical form, with
  the same database and the same settings. It comes back with 50 hits, every one
  of them *Gadus morhua*, topped by `MT456169.1`, the record the file was copied
  from. A form that answers a real barcode is a form that works.
- *Ruled out: the query is too short.* Both queries are 649 bases, and
  `mystery_fillet_1` filled its whole hit list at that length. So 649 bases is
  long enough to reach significance in this database.

That leaves the first reading, and this is the rare case where you can be sure
of it, because you know where the sequence came from. It's `mystery_fillet_1`
with the base order scrambled under a fixed seed, 20260802. For an unknown off a
real sample you'd never be that sure.

**Why.** The two searches differ in exactly one property, and that property is
history. Same length, same base composition, same database, same form. One is a
piece of an animal with relatives on deposit and the other is those same letters
in an order no animal has ever carried. Which is what a negative control buys
you. Any search that returns hits leaves one question open, and that question is
whether it would have returned hits regardless. You've now watched this form come
back empty, so you know it's capable of saying no.

**The most common wrong answer.** Reading the empty report as a failure of the
search rather than as a result. The If-it-breaks box in Chapter 7 says it plainly:
`No significant similarity found.` is the website reporting an outcome. Nothing
errored, nothing timed out, and there's nothing to retry. The instinct to press
the button again comes from the shape of the page, which looks like a page where
something went wrong.

The second most common is naming the right reading with no check behind it.
"The sequence has no relatives" is the correct sentence and it's worth nothing
on its own, because you'd have written the same sentence if you'd pasted a
protein into a nucleotide form or pointed the search at a database of one
organism. The exercise asks for the checks because the checks are what make the
sentence evidence.
