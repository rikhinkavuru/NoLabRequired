### Worked example: GSE60450

The question it's being tested against is Exercise 22.1's: do basal cells and
luminal cells differ in *Brca1* expression, measured as mean counts per million?

**How many samples?** Twelve.

```python
import pandas as pd

samples = pd.read_csv("data/GSE60450_samples.csv")
print(len(samples), "samples")
print(samples.groupby(["cell_type", "stage"]).size())
```

**Group structure, written as groups by samples per group.** Six groups by two
samples per group. Two cell types, basal and luminal, crossed with three stages,
virgin, pregnant and lactating. So every comparison inside this dataset is two
against two, unless you collapse a factor and make it six against six.

**One thing the metadata doesn't record.** Take your pick, and the interesting
one is the sequencing lane. The GEO sample records don't mention a lane anywhere.
It survives only because somebody left it inside a column heading in the count
file:

```
MCL1-DG_BC2CTUACXX_ACTTGA_L002_R1
```

`BC2CTUACXX` is the flowcell, `ACTTGA` is the index barcode, `L002` is the lane
and `R1` is the read number. Join it back to the labels on sample name rather
than on position:

```python
counts = pd.read_csv("data/GSE60450_counts.tsv.gz", sep="\t", nrows=1)
lanes = {c.split("_")[0]: c.split("_")[3] for c in counts.columns[2:]}
samples["lane"] = samples["sample"].map(lanes)
print(samples.groupby(["cell_type", "lane"]).size())
```

All six basal samples ran in lane 2 and all six luminal samples ran in lane 1.
The two move together, so a difference you measure between basal and luminal is
also a difference between two lanes, and no arithmetic afterwards pulls them
apart. Two more things the record doesn't hold and that you can't recover from a
column heading: how old the mice were in weeks, and when any library was
prepared.

**Size in bytes of the file you'd download.** 513,592 bytes for the count table,
compressed. That's 27,179 rows of genes by 12 columns of counts, out of samples
carrying between 20,015,386 and 24,723,827 counted reads each. A count is a
number and a read is four lines of text, which is the whole reason an experiment
this size fits in half a megabyte.

### The two you found yourself

The book's fact registry holds one dataset, and putting two more accessions on
this page with sample counts beside them would be inventing data. So here's how
to grade yours instead.

**Every group structure has to come off the record.** Not off your sense of how
the experiment must have been run. If you can't state it, write "group structure
not stated" beside that accession and keep the accession in your list. That's a
finding about the dataset rather than a failure of yours, and it's your reason to
go looking for a fourth.

**Then check yourself for the opposite problem.** If all three of your accessions
are the same organism and the same tissue, you searched one phrase and took the
first page of results. Two of the three should have arrived by a different route,
and the best route is a paper you already trust: papers print their accession in a
data availability statement, so read the paper and go straight to it rather than
searching GEO's free text.

**Four common outcomes that are results rather than failures.**

- The series lists samples and has no processed data file. That's a GEO record
  pointing at SRA, and nobody has done the counting. Nothing in this book can
  process a raw read. Cross it off and write down why.
- The download is one file per sample. Twelve samples means twelve files to read
  and join on gene identifier, which is a pandas job and probably an extra
  evening. Count that cost before you commit.
- The file is labeled controlled access. That's the TCGA tier boundary. The
  request goes through dbGaP and needs an institutional signing official, which
  you don't have. Filter your search to open-access data at the start.
- Two series use the word expression to mean two different things. One deposits
  raw counts, the next counts per million, the next FPKM, the next the log of one
  of those. Whole numbers with lots of zeros usually mean raw counts and decimals
  mean somebody normalized already. Treat that as a first read rather than proof.

**The most common wrong answer.** Skipping the fourth question, which is the one
that takes practice, and writing the first three off the record's front page. A
missing field rarely disqualifies a dataset. It caps what you may claim from that
dataset, and you want the cap written down before you have a result you like. The
lane confound above is the shape of the thing. It isn't on the GEO record at all,
and it's recoverable from a column heading. Once you know it's there, you know
that your basal-against-luminal comparison and a lane comparison are the same
comparison.

The second most common is writing the file size down after downloading it. The
chapter asks for it beside the accession before you fetch anything, because Joseph
from Chapter 1 is paying for that download by the gigabyte and a size read
afterwards is a receipt rather than a decision.
